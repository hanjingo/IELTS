There is a long-standing debate about whether or not people who have been in prison are better suited to talk to teenagers about the dangers of committing a crime. While some believe that they can offer invaluable insights and be effective mentors, others argue that their criminal pasts disqualify them from being credible role models. In this essay, I will explore both sides of the argument and ultimately conclude that people who have been in prison can indeed be effective speakers to teenagers.

On the one hand, opponents of using ex-convicts argue that their past mistakes will always cast doubt on their credibility. They contend that an ex-criminal who is preaching against the dangers of crime may be seen as a hypocrite, and that their words may be discounted by those who view their past behavior as proof that they do not practice what they preach. Additionally, some people may feel that their position as mentors makes them too close to the problem, and could lead vulnerable teenagers to believe that criminal activities are a viable option for their future.

On the other hand, supporters of ex-prisoners believe that the lessons they can offer are invaluable precisely because of their experiences. These people often argue that ex-convicts can better understand the mindset of someone who is considering committing a crime, which makes them better suited for the job. Additionally, many of these individuals have gone through substantial rehabilitation and counseling that has helped them address the underlying issues that once led them to a life of crime. This makes them uniquely qualified to offer advice, counsel and guidance to teenagers who are struggling with tough choices and challenges.

In conclusion, while some people may feel hesitant about using ex-convicts as speakers on the dangers of committing a crime, I believe that their experiences and insights can be a valuable resource for both teenagers and society at large. Rather than shaming or ostracizing those who have made mistakes in the past, we should seek to embrace their reform and progress, enabling them to pay it forward and use their mistakes as an instrument for good.
